#+TITLE: The NIST Manufacturing Control Hierarchies — Reference
#+STARTUP: overview

Not a Stone document. This records what the source material actually says, so
that when Stone borrows a term we can check the borrowing. Paraphrased from
the sources listed at the end, with the load-bearing sentences quoted.

*What it establishes.* Stone's containment axis — facility, production
control, composite, workstation, machine, device — is a hierarchy of
controllers in the shape NIST's manufacturing control projects arrived at, and
most of the words on that axis are theirs. There is no single NIST control
hierarchy, though. There are three lines of work, and they disagree about the
one question that matters most for the borrowing, whether the number of
levels is fixed:

- *AMRF* (NBS, from 1981): five fixed levels, each assigned specific manufacturing functions.
- *MSI* (NIST, 1990–93): NIST's own successor, which rejected AMRF's criteria for identifying a level and put any number of workcell levels between shop and equipment.
- *RCS* (Albus, from the mid-1970s, running alongside both): a layered reference model whose node design repeats at every layer, with the layer count set by the application.

The five fixed levels are AMRF's 1987 snapshot, and Stone does not use them.
What Stone reuses is the structure that survived AMRF's own revision, so this
entry leads with that and records the five levels compactly afterwards, so the
borrowing can still be checked. A second axis inside each level — regulation,
optimization, adaptation — is in [[file:multilayer-control.org][multilayer-control.org]]; what came after MSI
is in [[file:sima-activity-model.org][sima-activity-model.org]] and [[file:smart-manufacturing-ecosystem.org][smart-manufacturing-ecosystem.org]].

* The structure Stone reuses

Six things, each with the source it comes from.

** A hierarchy of controllers: command down, status up

AMRF's founding shape, kept by MSI and RCS. Jackson and Jones (1987): control
flow is strictly vertical and only between adjacent neighbours; each module
decomposes its supervisor's command into procedures it executes itself plus
subcommands to its subordinates, repeated down to primitive actions at the
lowest level; status feedback from subordinates closes the loop at each level.
A module's goals "must be consistent with those set by the module's supervisor
and they must commit the entire subordinate structure to a unified and
coordinated course of action" (§3.3).

In Stone this is ADR-001: One contract, all the way down — take a command,
report a result — and ADR-002's rule that state flows outward while parts
flow along routing.

** A level exists wherever coordination is needed, and the middle nests

MSI's revision of AMRF, NISTIR 4682 §3.4.2:

#+begin_quote
A level of control may be introduced whenever a coordinating or supervisory
function is needed. This typically occurs when the decomposition of the initial
problem regularly results in less complex, independent subproblems. ... In
particular, this means that there may be a different number of control levels,
depending upon the shop configuration.
#+end_quote

and NISTIR 4626 §2.3 on where the depth lives:

#+begin_quote
The grouping of equipment into workcells is based upon many factors and is
highly facility dependent. A workcell controller may control equipment
controllers directly, or may control any number of layers of workcell level
controllers.
#+end_quote

MSI's word for the thing that nests is *workcell*, and it is AMRF's cell and
workstation merged (§MSI below). Stone's composite is the same shape: a
composite presents itself to its parent as a workstation and nests to any
depth (ADR-020, F-24). The reason differs, and the difference matters. MSI's
depth is a property of the installation — machines that sit together need
coordinating. Stone's depth is a property of the plan — the work decomposes,
and someone wanted that decomposition accountable. A composite travels with
the work, which is what ADR-021 requires.

** A process plan is a template; a production plan binds it

MSI, NISTIR 4626 Appendix D:

#+begin_quote
process plan — A specification of tasks, or operations, to accomplish a given
goal — typically the manufacture of a product. In contrast to a production
plan, a process plan serves as a template, or recipe, for the accomplishment of
the tasks.

production plan — A specification of tasks, or operations, to accomplish a
specific goal, using specific resources at specific times.
#+end_quote

and on how a process plan names what it needs, NISTIR 4682 §3.5.1: "a process
plan will typically refer to required resources by class reference, as in
'This step requires a three-axis milling machine,' rather than 'This step
requires machine XYZ001.'" — with the footnote that the model "also supports
the referencing of resources by capability".

These are Stone's Process plan, Production plan and Capability (UL), and
ADR-021: A process plan names no resource. One difference: MSI has production
plans at shop, workcell and equipment level, and a workcell "is permitted to
generate its production plans on-line"; Stone has one production plan per
job, written in the facility.

** A checkpoint

MSI, NISTIR 4626 §2.4.1:

#+begin_quote
A checkpoint is a step in the production plan where the manufacturing process
may be halted in a safe manner without damaging either the equipment or the
workpiece and later replaced or resumed.
#+end_quote

Stone's Checkpoint (UL) is the boundary between two cycles of a machine
(ADR-016, ADR-033). MSI required the workcell to understand checkpoints in
1991 and the shop and workcell but not equipment in 1993, and admitted the
notion was not well defined above one device (§Where the sources disagree).
Stone requires it of every machine with no opt-out, which is the strictest of
the three answers and the one that buys "interruption costs at most one
cycle".

** A revision signal that carries no detail

MSI's *capability index*, NISTIR 4626 §3.1. Each subsystem carries a
monotonically increasing integer, incremented when a subordinate is lost or
added, or when "the capability index of a subordinate changes and the
subsystem controller determines that the capabilities of the whole subsystem
are thereby affected."

#+begin_quote
The administrative supervisor does not understand the capabilities of the
subsystem: it interprets a change in the capability index to mean that the
subsystem capabilities have changed in such a way as to require replanning for
its use. The capability index has no other intrinsic semantics.
#+end_quote

Each value must be distinguishable from all previous values, because the
supervisor may miss individual changes. This is Stone's Revision (UL) and
ADR-019: A revision bump is an opaque signal to replan, including the
conditional propagation upward.

** A guardian

MSI's console interface beside the supervisory and task ones, "allowing
external monitoring and intervention in the otherwise automatic operation of
the control entity" (NISTIR 4626 §4). It holds every administrative command
plus configuration commands — Attach, Detach, Ignore — and "has priority over
the supervisory interface". Any configuration change made through it
increments the capability index.

On who is behind it, 4626 §2.5: "the operator may be human, but is not
required to be, and may be an automated expert system, or a human with
computer assistance." NISTIR 5272 §2.6.1.1 keeps that in 1993, and adds a
*watchdog*, "a primitive guardian interface ... presumed always to exist for a
running control entity", described as a pair of red lights, one for planning
errors and one for job control errors, that stay on until the problems needing
assistance are resolved.

Stone's Guardian (UL) narrows this to the human alone, and ADR-022: Limits are
set from outside the job says why: an automated guardian would run inside the
job and could raise its own ceiling.

** Level for level

| Stone              | AMRF (1987)                                                                      | MSI (1991–93)                                                   |
|--------------------+----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Facility           | Facility: process planning, resource requirements, capital investment            | Not defined: "levels above Shop ... have not yet been defined"  |
| Production control | Shop: assigns and releases batch jobs to cells; allocates resources to them      | Shop: holds the workcells currently available; the highest level |
| Job / process plan | A batch job released to a cell                                                   | A production plan generated from a process plan                 |
| Composite          | Cell: routes jobs through workstations, replans on conflict; does not nest       | Workcell: "any number of layers"; AMRF's cell and workstation merged |
| Workstation        | Workstation: coordinates a grouping of equipment, including in-process inspection | Absorbed into the lowest workcell                               |
| Machine            | Equipment controller: translates the workstation's command, monitors execution   | Equipment controller: runs tasks on one device                  |
| Equipment          | The equipment level is the pair, controller plus device                          | —                                                               |
| Device             | The commercial machine with its own vendor controller                            | One per equipment controller                                    |

Two things in the table are easy to misread. AMRF's equipment level is not
Stone's device: it is a controller plus the device it drives (§AMRF below),
so it is Stone's machine and its equipment together. And Stone's workstation
is AMRF's, closely — "a small integrated physical grouping of equipment"
coordinated by one controller, whose sequence includes in-process inspection
— rather than something AMRF lacked.

* What Stone did not take

- /Five fixed levels/, each assigned specific manufacturing functions. AMRF's own successor rejected this by name, and Stone's levels are defined by containment: what a thing is depends on what contains it and what it contains.
- /Planning horizon as the criterion for a level./ AMRF's second guideline. Stone's horizons come out ordered anyway — a call in seconds, a cycle in seconds to a minute, a workstation in minutes, a composite in hours, process planning outside any job — but as a consequence, not the criterion. MSI rejected the horizon for the same kind of reason, calling it a hallmark of a correct decomposition rather than a test for one; Stone is closer to MSI than to AMRF here. What the principle was for is still worth knowing: it bounds the computation a level must do, and Stone's version of that problem is the cost of consulting the resident planner (ADR-010), where the principle would have argued for the same coarse default subscription that was chosen on cost grounds.
- /Two hierarchies, administrative and task/ (MSI 1991). MSI replaced this itself in 1993 with a planner/job-controller pair (§MSI below). Stone derives containment and routing from one written plan (ADR-020), which is a different cut.
- /An automated guardian./ Excluded by ADR-022.
- /Production plans at every level, generated on-line by a workcell./ Stone has one per job, and a resident planner that revises the process plan or re-dispatches its resources rather than a planner in every controller.
- /A scheduler./ AMRF puts a queue manager and a dispatch manager under every controller (§AMRF below), resequencing as status changes. Stone's combinators state precedence and a pool caps concurrency; nothing says which of the ready operations runs first.

* AMRF (NBS, 1981–87)

The Automated Manufacturing Research Facility was a small-batch manufacturing
testbed built at the US National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) from 1981, for
research into interface standards for automated factories. The control
architecture was a five-level command/feedback hierarchy.

** The three guidelines for introducing a level

Credited to Albus, Barbera and Nagel (1981):

1. Levels are introduced to reduce complexity and limit responsibility and authority.
2. Each level has a distinct *planning horizon*, which decreases as you go down the hierarchy.
3. Control resides at the lowest possible level.

/Attribution./ The list in this wording is Smith, Hoberecht and Joshi (1994)
summarising Albus et al.; the 1981 paper was not obtainable. Jackson and Jones
(1987) §3.3 elaborate the second: "The amount of computation that can be
performed in real-time at each level is limited by the planning horizon: the
period of time over which the module is responsible for planning and updating
local goals."

Stone keeps the first and third — containment limits responsibility, and the
escalation ladder of rework before routing before replanning before stopping
is control at the lowest level that can exercise it — and not the second.

** The five levels

Jackson and Jones (1987) §2.3: "the decomposition of the control functions
into five levels: facility, shop, cell, workstation, and equipment." Strictly
five; a cell is never a workstation.

- Facility :: The front office. Manufacturing engineering (CAD, group technology, process planning, with human involvement); information management (administrative and business functions); production management (long-range schedules, resource requirements, capital investment, quality summaries).
- Shop :: Coordinates production and support jobs on the floor and allocates resources to them. A task manager (capacity planning, batching orders, assigning and releasing batch jobs to cells, tracking orders) and a resource manager (allocating resources to cells, repair, procurement).
- Cell :: Sequences batch jobs of similar parts through workstations and supervises material handling and calibration; decides which workstation performs an operation, how tasks are scheduled across its workstations, and how parts and tools move between them. When a conflict arises at the workstation level, the cell replans, reroutes and reschedules. The AMRF cell was *virtual* — "dynamic production control structures which permit the time sharing of workstation level processing systems" — as against a "real" cell fixed on the floor.
- Workstation :: Directs "a small integrated physical grouping of equipment" — typically a robot, a machine tool, a material buffer and a control computer — through job setup, fixturing, cutting, chip removal, *in-process inspection*, takedown and cleanup. In-process inspection is distinct from the dedicated inspection station elsewhere in the shop.
- Equipment :: Front-end systems tied to individual commercial machines. The controller translates the workstation's commands into tasks the vendor-supplied controller understands, and monitors execution through the hardware's sensors. So the equipment /level/ is a pair: a controller plus a device with its own vendor controller beneath it.

Each level is data-driven — the data a module needs is held apart from its
control code — and data, unlike control, may be shared across levels.

** The Production Control module

One generic module, implemented at cell, workstation and equipment, itself a
three-level hierarchy:

- Production manager :: takes a job list and resources from the supervisor and status from the queue manager; on any change in job status, regenerates a real-time production plan — tasks per subordinate, precedence and coordination flags, start and finish times.
- Queue manager :: one per subordinate; resequences its queue as job status changes, passes command information down, removes completed jobs, reports timing upward.
- Dispatch manager :: one per subordinate; releases jobs to it and monitors them to completion, reporting delays.

Planning is the production manager and queue manager; control is the dispatch
manager.

Command carries an action verb (startup, shutdown, execution, maintenance,
wait), a pointer to a job list, and a pointer to a resource list; each job
entry has a new/old flag, an id, an action (plan, execute, cancel), a priority
and timing; each resource entry carries the supervisor's response and an
expected time of availability. Feedback mirrors it: operational status, job
status and resource requests, each a short condition code and a pointer to
detail.

** AMRF process plans

Used at the bottom three levels: machining and robot handling instructions,
workstation-level operations, cell-level routing steps, with alternate
sequences at each level. Each step carries a work element description,
precedence constraints and an estimated duration; plans carry an extended
bill of materials of tooling, fixtures, materials, systems, data and programs.

/Not verified./ A previous version of this entry described an AMRF process
plan as a directed graph whose nodes are operations and arcs are movements of
the part, built hierarchically so its levels of detail map onto the control
levels. That description is not in Jackson and Jones (1987); it is plausibly
from Jones and McLean (1986), which is paywalled and was not read. Treat it as
unattributed.

** Planning, scheduling, execution

Smith et al. (1994), crediting Joshi et al. (1990) and Jones and Saleh (1990):
"controller activities at each level in the hierarchy can be partitioned into
planning activities, scheduling activities, and execution activities" —
planning selects which tasks to perform, scheduling sets their start and
finish times at subordinates, execution verifies preconditions and carries
out the dialogue with subordinates. Coordination between levels is by
*generalized due dates*: "The shop sets a due date for the batch or part group
at each individual workstation that the parts visit", and within that
constraint each level schedules as it sees fit. Durations are negotiated
between a supervisor and its subordinates, never between peers.

* MSI (NIST, 1990–93)

Manufacturing Systems Integration was NIST's own successor project. Its
architecture document calls itself "a second generation AMRF architecture" and
says experience implementing AMRF "leads us to revise the hierarchical control
architecture of the AMRF as designed."

Three documents matter and are not interchangeable: NISTIR 4682 (the
architecture, September 1991), NISTIR 4626 (the control entity interfaces,
June 1991), and NISTIR 5272 (the revised interface specification, 1993, which
supersedes 4626).

** The objection to AMRF's levels

NISTIR 4682 §3.4.2 names the two AMRF criteria it rejects — span of control
("any complex of 7-plus-or-minus-2 controllers requires a level of control")
and the planning horizon:

#+begin_quote
The difficulty with these two criteria from the MSI point of view is that they
are not based on problem decomposition or activity coordination requirements.
... it is the difference in the nature and complexity of the problem
decomposition that determines the need for a level of control, not the number
of subordinates. ... these are not the criteria for determining the need for a
level of control, but rather (typical but not necessary) hallmarks of a
correct problem decomposition.
#+end_quote

Smith et al. (1994), from outside: "One of the main contentions of the MSI
group is that the five level hierarchy imposed by the original NIST
architecture is too rigid in structure."

** Three controller types

Equipment, workcell, shop. An equipment controller runs tasks on one device,
one task at a time; what is inside it is out of scope. A workcell is a
collection of equipment viewed as a functional unit under one supervisory
controller, and "A Workcell Level Controller (WLC) entity coordinates two or
more ELCs, WLCs or a combination of either type" (4682 §3.4.2). A shop holds
the workcells currently configured as available, and is the highest level:
"higher levels of control above the Shop Level, which would deal with business
and management concerns and coordination of multiple shops, have not yet been
defined." AMRF's facility level was dropped, not revised.

On the word: "The notion of control in this level has similarities both to
the AMRF Virtual Cell and Workstation level concepts, hence the term workcell.
In fact, the lowest level workcell, which supervises a collection of
equipment, corresponds to the AMRF Workstation level of control" (4682
§3.4.2).

** How a control entity is structured: 1991 and 1993

In 1991 (4626 §2.3–2.4, 4682 §3.6) a controller had two hierarchies over it.
The *administrative* hierarchy — at most one supervisor, any number of
subordinates — carries startup, shutdown, health and configuration and
"remains unchanged during the manufacturing of tasks". The *task* hierarchy is
a client/server relation, "continually redefined depending on which tasks are
outstanding", and "task control is not required to follow the administrative
control paths." A controller could be asked to do work by something other
than its administrative supervisor.

By 1993 this framing is gone. NISTIR 5272 §2.2.2 splits a control entity into
a *planner* and a *job controller* and defines five interfaces: planning, job
control, planning-to-job-control, guardian planning, guardian job control.
Every control entity above equipment holds the pair.

Where the planner sits is left open. 5272 §2.2.3 puts two requirements on
an implementation — "Each job controller in the control hierarchy has some
agent which is responsible for creating and maintaining its schedule" and
"some agent which is responsible for addressing planning errors which the job
controller detects" — and then names three valid placements and allows
hybrids: an *embedded planner* inside each control entity; a *distributed
planning hierarchy* "which mirrors the control hierarchy (i.e., a planner for
each job controller)"; and a *centralized planner* "which plans for every job
controller in the control hierarchy", where "Each control entity is a
conceptual entity only; there is no physical correspondence." A planner
"supports resource allocation and scheduling; it may, but is not required
to, support process planning and batching" (§2.2.2). Its two functions are
"to schedule the execution of tasks for specific job controller(s) and to
assist in anomaly recovery on behalf of those job controller(s)" (§2.4).

Plans are three kinds in 5272 (§2.3.1), not two: a *process plan* is the
generic recipe; a *production-managed plan* expands it for a required number
of products on a given factory configuration, carrying "decisions about part
mix, batching, lot sizes and material handling"; a *production plan* refines
that with specific resources and times per step. Production-managed plans
are the planner's input and production plans its output; production plans
are the job controller's input. Both of the latter "must be hierarchical"
(§2.3.2): a shop-level plan's steps refer to workcells and subordinate plans,
down to an equipment-level plan "which contains only primitive operations".

The job controller runs a *token of control* (§2.5): it executes the steps
assigned to it, and on a step referring to a subordinate passes the token
down by commanding that subordinate, monitoring it by status until the token
returns on normal or abnormal completion. "There will be multiple tokens of
control for a task if there are parallel or concurrent paths in that
production plan (one for each path)."

Controllers are also typed by what they can do with tasks rather than by
position (4626 §2.4.2): one that accepts a task at a time and starts it at
once; one that queues tasks and starts them on its own schedule without
understanding production plans; one that queues, understands production plans
and schedules, and reports schedule violations. MSI's testbed interfaced a
single-task controller at the equipment level and a queueing one at the
workcell level "as it had its own embedded scheduling capabilities."

** Error recovery, replanning and covenants

NISTIR 4626 §2.5: a task failure that cannot be handled locally is reported in
task status to the client and ultimately reaches the shop controller, which
"alerts the Production Planner to the need for replanning". Contingency
production plans may come from existing alternative process plans or from new
ones generated on-line.

NISTIR 5272 §2.4.2 and §2.6 make this *anomaly* handling — resource, task and
tooling anomalies — with two mechanisms: human intervention, and *replanning*,
"the dynamic modification of production plans", which "may involve
rescheduling steps, adding, removing, or modifying steps, or even taking
different production alternatives". 5272 is candid that "with current
technology, it is likely that a planner will only be able to resolve problems
that can be solved by rescheduling steps."

5272 also introduces *covenants*: a supervisor asks subordinates who can
perform an operation, each bids, the supervisor accepts one, and the accepted
bid becomes a contract, each associated with one production plan.

** Checkpoints, in both years

In 4626 the workcell controller must understand checkpoints, and Pause and
Terminate both act at the next one. 4626 §8 then admits:

#+begin_quote
Checkpointing a task at a lower level in the control hierarchy necessarily
means that a higher level controller (namely the task client) is in the middle
of a manufacturing step, and is not considered checkpointed on that task. The
current meaning of checkpoint is not well defined for any level other than the
Equipment Level.
#+end_quote

4682 Appendix 3 records the same from the 1990 implementation: "The notion of
checkpointing is clear for Equipment Level plans, but requires refinement for
higher level plans." 5272 inverts the requirement rather than solving it:
"The shop level planner and job controller and all workcell planners and job
controllers are required to understand checkpoints. ... The MSI architecture
does not require that equipment job controllers understand the notion of
checkpoint." Its definition also loosens, from halted "and later replaced or
resumed" to "temporarily or indefinitely suspended in a safe manner without
damage".

* RCS (Albus, mid-1970s onward)

Albus's own line ran alongside AMRF and outlived it. RCS-2, built for the
AMRF, "was used to define an eight level hierarchy consisting of Servo,
Coordinate Transform, E-Move, Task, Workstation, Cell, Shop, and Facility
levels of control. Only the first six levels were actually built." Later
generations added a world model, an operator interface and a value judgment
system.

On depth, RCS is not MSI. Its layers are typed by characteristic timing —
"The functionality of each level in the control hierarchy can be derived from
the characteristic timing of that level, and vice versa" — which is AMRF's
second guideline, kept. Layers may be absent in a branch: "the missing modules
can be thought of as degenerate computing nodes that produce unity gain
pass-through of inputs to outputs." Albus's word *recursive* names the node
design repeating at every layer, not variable depth, and he answers the
rigidity charge directly: "A reference model architecture is a canonical
form, not a system design specification."

* Where the sources disagree

Five places. Each is a point where borrowing from "the NIST taxonomy" means
choosing which NIST.

- Whether the number of levels is fixed :: AMRF: five. MSI: "a different number of control levels, depending upon the shop configuration". RCS: set by the application, with layers allowed to be absent in a branch.
- What identifies a level :: AMRF: span of control and planning horizon. MSI: problem decomposition — a level exists "whenever a coordinating or supervisory function is needed". RCS: the planning horizon, kept throughout.
- Whether cell and workstation are two kinds :: AMRF: two, and a cell is never a workstation. MSI: one kind, the workcell, with the lowest workcell being AMRF's workstation.
- Who must understand a checkpoint :: 4626 (1991): the workcell, admitting the notion is only well defined at equipment. 5272 (1993): shop and workcell, and equipment need not. MSI moved the obligation upward rather than solving it.
- How a controller is structured :: 1991: administrative and task hierarchies. 1993: a planner/job-controller pair and five interfaces, with covenants and anomalies. The capability index does not appear in 5272; whether it was dropped or renamed was not established.

* What the sources do not name

None of the sources names a "nested cell pattern" or any equivalent phrase.
MSI's own word for the thing that nests is *workcell level*, and its phrasing
for the depth is "any number of layers of workcell level controllers" (4626)
and "any number of 'levels' of workcell management within a control
hierarchy" (5272). Smith et al. (1994), from outside: "A hierarchical
structure of arbitrary depth can be constructed using these three types of
controllers." Albus writes *recursive* of the node design, not the depth. The
idea is in the sources three times over; the phrase is not, and anything
written here beyond the sources' own words is ours.
